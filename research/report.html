Sampling and convention dependence in lunar polar illumination estimates from public DEMs

A reproducibility note for SUNLOCK · 15 November 2026 · code and data: github.com/shahaman098/sunlock

Abstract

Illumination percentages for the lunar south pole are widely quoted (≈ 80–88%) but are computed under conventions that are rarely stated together: terrain resolution, ray-sampling interval, observer height, temporal baseline, and whether the Sun's disc centre or any part of its disc is required to clear the horizon. Using only public data — the LRO LOLA gridded elevation models at 80 m, 40 m and 20 m per pixel — we show that at a single coordinate (89.438°S, 222.717°E) the time-averaged illumination estimate varies from 36.8% to 94.4% depending only on the distance between horizon ray samples. With bilinear interpolation the estimator converges to ≈ 54–59% (disc centre) or ≈ 67–71% (any part of disc). We further verify the ephemeris and lunar rotation model against JPL Horizons to 0.0032° in sub-solar longitude, so the spread is attributable to terrain sampling and convention rather than to astronomy. We conclude that a single-number illumination claim is meaningless unless its sampling convention and criterion are stated, and we recommend the convention used by Gläser et al. (2017) — any part of the solar disc, converged sampling, searched cluster coordinates — for comparability with the literature.

1. Introduction

Mission concepts for the lunar south pole depend on two quantities: how often the Sun is visible, and how often Earth is in view. Published values cluster around 80–88% at the best sites (Mazarico et al., 2011; Gläser et al., 2017), with continuous darkness of a few days. Those numbers are computed from digital terrain models (DTMs) by ray-marching a local horizon and testing the Sun against it.

Between the DTM and the percentage sit several choices that are usually reported separately, if at all. This note isolates them on one coordinate, using the same ephemeris and the same code path throughout, so that the only variables are terrain sampling and the visibility criterion.

3. Method

For a coordinate, the horizon is the supremum of elevation angle along each of 360 azimuths:

slope_i = (h_i − h_0 − d_i² / 2R) / d_i        horizon = max_i atan(slope_i)

where d_i is the horizontal distance to sample i and the d²/2R term is the lunar curvature drop. A point is illuminated when the solar elevation exceeds the horizon in the Sun's azimuth, integrated over 1 Jan – 30 Jun 2027 at 30-minute steps.

Three variants are compared:

  • nearest: sample the nearest raster cell at a fixed distance interval;
  • bilinear: interpolate between cells, which is well-posed as the interval → 0;
  • criterion: Sun centre clears the horizon, vs any part of the 0.266° disc.

4. Validation of the astronomy

Before attributing any spread to terrain, the sky must be shown correct. At 12 epochs in 2027, against JPL Horizons:

The sub-solar comparison is the important one: it tests the rotation model, not just the ephemeris. An earlier implementation using only the polynomial prime meridian (38.3213 + 13.17635815 d) was 2.24° off and drifting 0.6°/yr, while its latitude matched to 0.001° — a failure mode that a latitude-only check would not catch.

6. Discussion

The literature is not wrong, and neither is the shipped number — they measure different things.

Gläser et al. (2017) define illumination as "the percentage of a given period in which any portion of the Sun is visible", at 2 m above ground, over twenty years, using a 20 m/px DTM, at clusters found by search and only "twenty to forty meters wide". Mazarico et al. (2011) used a 240 m/px DTM over the same twenty years. Speyerer and Robinson (2013) used 100 m/px imagery over one year. The resolution spread alone is a factor of twelve.

Our shipped 86% is disc-centre, nearest-neighbour at 160 m, at a region-centre coordinate. It sits inside the published band by coincidence of two offsetting approximations: coarse sampling inflates by roughly 15 points, the stricter criterion deflates by 8–12.

The one genuinely comparable quantity is the longest continuous darkness at the Shackleton peak — 3.5 days — inside the "less than three and up to eight days" reported by Gläser et al.

A related subtlety is worth separating explicitly. Gläser et al. argue that coarser imagery underestimates illumination because "small-scale illuminated areas less than 100 meters across" are unresolved. That statement concerns the illuminated area fraction at an instant. At a point, coarse sampling has the opposite effect, because it misses small obstructions. Both are true; they are different metrics, and conflating them is a common source of apparent disagreement.

7. Limitations

  • One coordinate, one hemisphere-season (180 days). A twenty-year baseline would sample

worse seasons.

  • No comparison against a published figure at the same coordinates and criterion — the

papers do not publish per-coordinate tables in machine-readable form.

  • Nearest-neighbour ray marching is aliased by construction; the bilinear variant used here

reduces but does not eliminate that.

  • No thermal, dust, or power-system modelling; no terrain self-shadowing between distant

peaks beyond the 64 km march.

8. Conclusions

1. A lunar polar illumination percentage is only meaningful with its sampling interval, terrain resolution, observer height, temporal baseline and disc criterion stated. 2. At one south-pole coordinate, varying only the ray-sampling interval moves the answer between 36.8% and 94.4%. 3. The frame and ephemeris are not the source: they agree with JPL Horizons to 0.0032°. 4. For comparability, adopt the literature convention — any part of the solar disc, converged interpolation, searched cluster coordinates — and report the shadow-duration statistic, which is far more stable than the illumination percentage (3.5 days here vs 3–8 days published).
